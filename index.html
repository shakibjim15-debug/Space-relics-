Two worlds, one long goodbye

Some missions never come home.

NASA left descent stages, landers, rovers, experiments and an aircraft on two worlds. This atlas follows the hardware that stayed — and the science it still makes possible.

Scroll to begin
01 01 / Tranquility Base

One small step, one descent stage

Apollo 11's Eagle touched down with only seconds of hover fuel to spare. The ascent stage had to be light enough to lift the crew home, so the descent stage, the Laser Ranging Retroreflector and the early experiments stayed in the dust.

02 02 / The rover years

A field site with parked machines

Apollo 15, 16 and 17 carried a lunar rover and a longer scientific payload. Each crew left a descent stage, a rover and an ALSEP behind. The Moon became a museum of working hardware.

03 03 / First robots on Mars

Landers that never came home

Viking 1 and 2 arrived in 1976 and kept working for years. In 1997, Pathfinder bounced to a stop and released Sojourner. Neither mission had a return trip; both changed how we read the Martian surface.

04 04 / The long goodbye

Built for months, remembered for decades

Spirit, Opportunity, Phoenix and InSight outlived their planned missions, then failed for very Martian reasons: dust, cold and winter light. Their last signals mark the end of a mission, not the end of their usefulness.

05 05 / Ingenuity

Seventy-two flights, one last field

Ingenuity was meant to fly five times. It made 72 flights before a hard landing damaged its rotors in January 2024. The helicopter remains in Jezero Crater, the first aircraft left behind on another planet.

Coda

What remains is not just junk

A retroreflector still answers lasers from Earth. A seismometer's data still describes marsquakes. Rover tracks and descent stages mark the places where our maps of two worlds became ground truth.
